E2 is concerted — the base pulls off a β-hydrogen at the exact instant the leaving group departs and a new π bond forms. That only works from certain conformations. Rotate the molecule below to find out which ones.

:B⁻ + H–Cβ–Cα–LG  →  B–H + Cβ=Cα + LG⁻
one step · one transition state · geometry-dependent

01 — Why geometry matters

Three things happen at once

In the E2 transition state, the C–H bond and the C–LG bond are both partway broken while a π bond is partway formed between the two carbons. That developing π bond is built from the p-orbital character left behind as each σ bond breaks — and p orbitals only overlap into a π bond if they're pointed the same way.

The curved arrows show where the electrons go: the base's lone pair forms the new B–H bond (teal), the breaking C–H bond's electrons swing in to form the π bond (amber), and the C–Br bond's electrons leave entirely with the bromide (red). All four bonds are only partly formed or broken at once — shown in brackets with a double-dagger (‡), the standard notation for a transition state — and negative charge builds up on both B and Br as they pull electron density toward themselves.

Anti-periplanar

180°

H and the leaving group point in opposite directions. The conformation is staggered — no eclipsing strain — and the breaking σ bonds line up for clean backside overlap. The dominant pathway for open-chain E2.

Syn-periplanar

0° / 360°

H and the leaving group point the same direction. Orbital alignment is possible, but the conformation is eclipsed. Higher energy — usually only seen in ring systems where anti is geometrically locked out.

Gauche

~60°, 120°, 240°, 300°

The C–H and C–LG bonds sit at an angle to each other with no useful overlap. There's no path to a π bond from here — a concerted E2 simply can't happen at this dihedral.
